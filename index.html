Selected Research Projects

Housing Price Inequality and Urban Segregation

This project investigates spatial inequality in housing markets across major European cities. Using panel data from 2010–2024, I estimate hedonic price models to identify how proximity to transport infrastructure, school quality, and income distribution affect housing prices. The analysis applies fixed effects regression to control for unobserved heterogeneity at the city level. Results suggest that transport accessibility has a significantly larger effect on prices in high-income districts, reinforcing existing patterns of spatial inequality.

Labour Market Effects of Minimum Wage Increases

This study evaluates the impact of minimum wage reforms on employment outcomes using a difference-in-differences approach. The dataset includes firm-level employment records across Germany and France between 2012 and 2022. I construct treatment and control groups based on exposure to policy changes and estimate heterogeneous effects across sectors. Findings indicate modest negative employment effects in low-productivity firms, but no significant aggregate employment loss, suggesting partial adjustment through productivity gains and price pass-through.

Inflation Dynamics and Energy Price Shocks in Europe

This project analyses the transmission of energy price shocks into headline inflation across Eurozone countries. Using a vector autoregression (VAR) framework, I decompose inflation into core and energy-driven components. The results show that energy price shocks have short-run but persistent medium-term effects on inflation expectations, particularly in economies with weaker monetary transmission mechanisms. The study also highlights cross-country heterogeneity in inflation sensitivity to global commodity markets.

Public Transport Investment and Labour Mobility

This research examines how expansions in public transport infrastructure affect regional labour mobility. Using geospatial data combined with labour force surveys, I estimate how commuting times influence job matching efficiency. The results suggest that reduced commuting times increase labour market participation and widen job search radius, particularly among lower-income workers. The findings support the hypothesis that transport investment has significant indirect effects on labour market efficiency.
